Does a lower game price mean more sales?

Steam data pulled 6 November 2026. Sales are Gamalytic estimates. Prices are the listed price at that date, in US dollars.

As a strict rule, no. For the same game, usually yes.

Across Steam, cheaper games do not reliably sell more. The cheaper game sold more in only 44.6% of closely matched pairs, and median sales rise with price from $4.99 to $59.99.

Price still matters. The studies that isolate price find that sales rise by more than the size of a cut, so a small permanent cut would raise revenue. The size of the effect also depends on how the cut is run: Valve reported flat revenue from quiet changes and 40 times the revenue from one promoted sale.

Test 1: Does the cheaper game sell more?

A strict rule predicts the cheaper game wins every comparison, so I compared pairs of games with different prices. Unless a row says otherwise, each pair shares a publisher class, release year, main tag and 10-point review band. Hobbyist publishers are left out.

Test 3: What happens when price changes for the same game?

This is the question that matters most for pricing decisions, and the Steam snapshot above cannot answer it. These sources address it more directly.

Elasticity is the % change in sales from a 1% price change, shown here as a magnitude.
SourceFindingElasticity
Cao & Wang, 2024Demand model on weekly Steam data, 2019 to 2021. Working paper, games priced $1 to $50 and ranked in the top 10,000.about 2.2
DerdengerDemand model on PS2-era console games, for a permanent 1% price cut.about 1.5
Valve, 2011Gabe Newell on quiet price changes: "pricing was perfectly elastic". One write-up says lower prices brought more sales, higher prices fewer, and gross revenue stayed the same.about 1

At an elasticity of 2.2, a 10% cut gives about 25% more copies and about 12% more revenue. At 1.5, it gives about 17% more copies and 5% more revenue. At 1, revenue stays flat. A claim that doubling the price costs only 20% of sales works out to about 0.32, well below all three.

Promoted sales are much larger and also inconsistent:

  • Newell, 2011: "our gross revenue increased by a factor of 40" in a sale, "completely not predicted" by the quiet tests. One write-up gives the discount as 75% and says "sales", not revenue.
  • Valve's 2009 figures for a 16-day holiday sale, in dollars: 10% off gave +35%, 25% off +245%, 50% off +320% and 75% off +1,470%. Coding Horror lists the same numbers.
  • Left 4 Dead at half price for a weekend: sales up 3,000%, and Newell said revenue beat its launch.

Temporary sales overstate what a lasting cut would do, since some buyers wait for them. Cao & Wang also find that discounts lift later sales through extra reviews, so a price cut's effect is not limited to the sale itself.

How the Steam estimates moved

A regression of log copies on log price, across games, gives very different answers depending on who is included. That is why it should not be read as the effect of price.

The last row shows the sample trap. Keeping only games above a revenue line forces cheap games in the sample to have high sales, which flips the sign. Publisher class may also partly reflect a game's sales, which would affect the rows that control for it.

What this cannot prove

  • Sales are estimates. Gamalytic copy counts appear to be modelled, not reported, and I have not checked them against publisher figures.
  • Prices are a snapshot. The data has today's list price, which may differ from launch price, and most copies of older games may have sold at a discount.
  • Steam only. Console, mobile and other PC stores are not covered in the main data.
  • The same-game evidence is thin. The two papers are models, and the Steam one is a working paper. Valve's numbers are old, reported by the press, and come from a company with an interest in sales. I found no controlled test of a lasting price change with published sales numbers.
  • Not every source could be read directly. The original GeekWire report of Newell's 2011 talk and the Left 4 Dead piece on Co-Optimus blocked automated access, so those quotes come from the outlets linked above.

Data and method

All 137 pages of Gamalytic's public game list, 136,346 games. I removed 19,587 free games and games with no sales or release date, leaving 116,440 paid games. Its robots.txt allowed crawling. Gross is list price × estimated copies.
